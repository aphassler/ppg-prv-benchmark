Benchmark report · finger PPG at rest

Beat Detectors at the PRV Floor

Five photoplethysmography beat detectors, run end-to-end against a synchronous ECG gold standard and scored on the errors that matter for pulse rate variability. Across 13,859 reference beats, beat-detection F1 spanned 0.7 percentage points while the RMSSD error it is supposed to predict spanned a factor of nine — and the best detector's beat-to-beat timing error was indistinguishable from the physiological floor beneath it.

Why this benchmark exists

F1 is measured; RMSSD is what people use

The standard open benchmarks for PPG beat detection — Charlton et al. 2022 and 2025 above all — report beat-detection F1 and heart-rate MAPE. Almost none report beat-to-beat interval RMSE, or the error in the HRV indices that actually get consumed downstream.

That gap is not cosmetic. F1 is scored with a ±150 ms matching tolerance, which is roughly five times a typical resting RMSSD. A detector can place every beat inside that window — scoring a flawless F1 — while its intervals are useless for the successive-difference metrics that clinical and consumer PRV depends on. This benchmark closes the gap for one well-defined regime: finger PPG, seated, 100 Hz.

The headline

F1 saturates before it can rank anything

At rest on clean finger PPG every detector lands within a fraction of a percentage point of every other. The metric has no dynamic range left to separate them, while the error that matters spreads across most of an order of magnitude.

The floor

The detector contributes essentially nothing

A constant pulse arrival time cancels inside an interval, so whatever PPI − RRI error remains is detector jitter combined with beat-to-beat variation in pulse arrival time — physiology, not a bug. Separating the two needs an estimate of the irreducible part: the median interval across all six detectors, keyed per reference beat, forms a consensus fiducial whose residual error is dominated by the physiology rather than by any one algorithm.

That consensus floor came to 6.92 ms RMSE. The best detector's IBI RMSE was 6.92 ms — an excess over the floor of +0.00 ms, with the whole field inside 0.36 ms of it. On resting finger PPG the choice of beat detector adds nothing measurable to interval timing error. The ~7 ms is the gap between pulse rate variability and heart rate variability, and no better algorithm closes it.

This is an estimate rather than ground truth: it assumes detector errors are independent enough for the median to cancel them. It is the reason the leaderboard below is worth reading for its HRV columns and not its IBI column.

Read the qppg row. Under the Kubios-style Lipponen–Tarvainen correction that both reports recommend as the default, its RMSSD error is 13.98 ms — the worst in the field by a factor of nine. Under a plain local median/MAD rule the same detector, on the same signal, posts 2.11 ms and the best IBI RMSE of any detector tested. The apparent quality of a detector is not separable from what repairs its mistakes.

The mechanism is specific. qppg misses roughly 4 % of beats; each miss leaves a doubled interval that passes any plausible physiological screen at 70 bpm, and each doubled interval corrupts two successive differences. Three such intervals in 582 inflated one record's RMSSD from 30 ms to 88 ms.

Lipponen–Tarvainen did not repair them. On a controlled synthetic series with three deliberately deleted beats the same NeuroKit2 implementation inserts all three correctly, so this is not a wiring fault — on real data at realistic variability its relative thresholds let the doubled intervals through. It also raised IBI RMSE for every detector (6.06 → 6.92 ms and worse), because replacing beats at interpolated positions moves them away from the true ECG beats. Treat this as an observation about one implementation on one dataset, not a refutation of the method — Kubios's own implementation may differ.

Experiment E1 · fiducial point

The systolic apex is a catastrophic place to measure

Peralta et al. (2019) found the mid-amplitude point, the first-derivative apex and the tangent intersection superior to the apex and the foot. On this data the effect is far larger than that literature suggests — the apex is not merely second-best, it is unusable.

The apex of a PPG pulse is broad and flat, so its argmax wanders with noise; the systolic upslope is steep and its half-amplitude crossing is well-conditioned. The winner — the mid-upslope — is MSPTDfast v2's own published fiducial, which vindicates that design choice. The compass report's recommendation to use the maximum of the first derivative lands second, not first.

Experiment E2 · sampling rate

Sub-sample interpolation erases the sample grid

At the 100 Hz operating rate one sample is 10 ms — the same order as the RMSSD being measured. Placing a beat on that grid is uniform quantisation with timing SD = T/√12, and an interval is the difference of two independently quantised beats, scaling that by √2. Adding the measured floor in quadrature gives a prediction with no fitted parameters.

Two consequences. First, the 100 Hz operating point costs nothing: with sub-sample refinement it matches the 500 Hz diagnostic ceiling to within 0.01 ms. Second, the degradation without interpolation is entirely quantisation — the theory predicts every point to within a quarter of a millisecond, which means nothing else about low-rate sampling is hurting the measurement.

This also sharpens the compass report's smartphone claim. It puts a ±16.6 ms ambiguity and a 16–20 ms PRV floor on 30 fps camera PPG that “no amount of interpolation can completely recover.” The quantisation arithmetic is right — 14.83 ms measured here at 30 Hz — but the conclusion is not: interpolation recovered it to 6.15 ms. For contact PPG the 30 fps floor is a solved problem. Camera PPG may still be floored by its far worse SNR, which this dataset cannot speak to.

Fact-check

What the source reports got wrong

Both reports were checked against primary sources. Their shared core recommendation — MSPTDfast v2, a mid-upslope fiducial, explicit quality gating, conservative beat correction — survives, and E1 and the leaderboard support it. Seven claims did not survive.

01

“RMSSD and HF power are systematically overestimated by PPG-derived PRV.”

The sign is inverted. The largest study either report cites (Kantrowitz et al. 2025, n = 931) found PRV underestimates: SDNN −13.1 ms, rMSSD −5.6 ms, pNN50 −3.9 %, all p < 0.00001. The direction is context-dependent, not systematic.

“RMSSD bias 3–4 ms and SDNN bias 7–12 ms.”

Attributed to the same study, whose published overall biases are −5.6 ms and −13.1 ms.

That study used an upper-arm/bicep armband. Neither report says so, which materially weakens it as evidence for the measurement sites they are actually discussing.

A “43-study meta-analysis” and “Xu et al. 2026 systematic review” cited as two independent sources.

They are the same paper (Sensors 26:5192). Neither report noticed. Its pooled estimate also rests on the 10 studies with sufficient data, not 43, which is the qualitative-synthesis count.

05

“Upsample the PPG (cubic spline to ≥250 Hz) before fiducial detection. Detect beats with MSPTDfast.”

Self-defeating: MSPTDfast's entire speed advantage is downsampling to 20 Hz internally. Detect at the low rate, refine the fiducial against the high-rate grid. E2 shows the refinement is what carries the accuracy anyway.

06

“Locate the fiducial at the maximum of the first derivative.”

Sound advice, but second-best and presented as settled. MSPTDfast v2 as published emits a mid-upslope point, and E1 measures that as better: 6.06 ms against 6.87 ms IBI RMSE. The pairing the report recommends is not a published configuration.

07

Lipponen–Tarvainen recommended as the default correction.

The strongest claim in either report about correction is right in direction and wrong in specifics. Correction is the largest lever — qppg's RMSSD error falls from 23.81 ms to 2.11 ms — but the recommended method was beaten by a plain local median/MAD rule on every detector's IBI RMSE and on four of six RMSSD errors, and it failed outright on the missed-beat case.

Also flagged and not relied upon: the PPG-PT transformer's F1 98.04 %, all smartphone-camera figures, Huang's DAG result, and Watanabe's claimed “145 ms PRV error reduction,” which is implausibly large. Confirmed against primary sources: MSPTDfast v2's design parameters and F1 scores, its 5.3–35.9 % execution time, the Charlton 2022 benchmark structure, Peralta's fiducial ranking, and the Xu pooled errors.

Method

How it was measured

Every approach is a full pipeline, not a detector. Only the detector stage differs, so differences are attributable.

  1. 01Decimate 500 → 100 Hz, anti-aliased, DC removed first.
  2. 02Band-pass 0.5–8 Hz, 4th-order Butterworth, zero-phase.
  3. 03Detect beats — the stage under test.
  4. 04Locate the fiducial and refine it below the sample grid.
  5. 05Gate on quality — skewness SQI, swept across coverage levels.
  6. 06Correct intervals — none, median/MAD, or Lipponen–Tarvainen.
  7. 07Compute HRV — time, frequency (Welch and Lomb–Scargle), non-linear.

Beats are matched to the ECG annotations one-to-one within ±150 ms after removing a per-record constant pulse arrival time (median 274 ms, range 234–315 ms). Intervals count only where beats are consecutive in both series, so a missed beat is scored as a detection failure rather than smuggled in as a timing failure. Coverage is swept at 100 / 90 / 75 / 50 % and the leaderboard is read at matched coverage, so no detector can win by abstaining on the hard segments.

Two defects surfaced in QC that would each have produced a plausible-looking but wrong benchmark. The raw channels are transmission pulse oximetry and arrive inverted — more blood means a lower sample value — so every fiducial would have landed on the diastolic decay while F1 still looked fine; polarity is now detected automatically from first-derivative skew and confirmed against morphology and physiological PAT on all 22 records. And scipy.signal.decimate explodes on the ~74,000 DC pedestal, producing an edge transient 44 standard deviations tall that swamped every amplitude threshold and made four of the six detectors return zero beats.

Reproduction cost

The whole benchmark now runs in nine seconds

The first complete sweep took about 14 minutes. It now takes 9.3 s, and the four experiments 13.0 s — a 90× and 115× reduction, with every one of ~112,000 field comparisons against the original results agreeing to within 1×10−9.

The GPU was measured out rather than assumed away. The scalogram is the only large data-parallel kernel in the pipeline — 404 M boolean comparisons for the entire benchmark, about 40 µs of arithmetic on an RTX A4000 and comfortably resident in 16 GB. But it is 10.5 % of the remaining runtime, so making it free would take 9.3 s to 8.3 s. Everything else — Lomb–Scargle over ~600 irregular points, greedy beat matching, branchy artifact classification — is small-array, data-dependent work that a GPU is the wrong shape for. Memory was never a constraint either: 20 workers hold ~7 MB of signal each against 29 GB available.

One result changed, and it is worth stating. MSPTD's cost was 0.89 s per minute of signal using NeuroKit2's reference implementation, which populates the scalogram with a doubly nested Python loop. Vectorised — bit-identical output, asserted per window against the reference — it runs 30× faster, at 0.03 s/min.

That reframes the published efficiency claim. Against the reference implementation, MSPTDfast v2 measured here at 2.3 % of MSPTD's cost, consistent with Charlton's reported 5.3–35.9 %. Against a vectorised MSPTD it measures 68 %. Most of the advantage visible in Python is interpreter overhead in the reference code, not the algorithmic reduction in scale count. The algorithmic saving is real — MSPTDfast does roughly 47× less scalogram work — but the like-for-like ratio remains unmeasured, because MSPTDfast itself still runs the reference loops here.

Scope

What this does not show

22 healthy adults, mean age 28.5, finger PPG, seated, high SNR, mean heart rate 54–121 bpm, reference SDNN 18–127 ms. This is a favourable case and the numbers are an upper bound, not a free-living expectation.

Nothing here transfers automatically to wrist PPG, ambulatory recording, camera PPG, arrhythmia, or clinical populations. The finding that detector choice is irrelevant is a finding about this regime: it is precisely where the published F1 tables are all clustered above 99 %, and the same detectors separate sharply under exercise in Charlton's own benchmark. Where beats are hard to find, finding them is the problem; where they are easy, everything downstream is.

pyPPG (Aboy++), which one source report rates the strongest alternative, is absent: it pins Python 3.10 with numpy 1.23 and will not install alongside this stack.

PRV is not HRV. Results are reported as pulse rate variability throughout.
